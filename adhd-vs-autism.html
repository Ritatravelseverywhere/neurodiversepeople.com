ADHD vs autism — what's the difference?

ADHD and autism share many surface traits — distractibility, social difficulties, emotional intensity. But they're distinct conditions with different underlying mechanisms. Here's how to tell them apart, and what it means when both are present.

9 minute read  ·  NeuroDiverse People

Why ADHD and autism are so often confused

Both ADHD and autism are neurodevelopmental conditions — they affect how the brain develops and processes information. Both are present from birth, both affect attention and social functioning, and both are frequently missed in women and girls.

Because they share so many surface-level traits, they're regularly mistaken for each other — both by individuals trying to understand themselves and by clinicians who don't specialise in neurodevelopmental conditions. A study by Young et al. found that autistic women are significantly more likely to receive an ADHD diagnosis before their autism is identified, and vice versa.

The key difference — why it matters

The most useful way to understand the core difference:

  • ADHD is primarily a difference in attention regulation and impulse control, rooted in dopamine and norepinephrine dysregulation. The fundamental struggle is getting the brain to focus on the right things at the right time.
  • Autism is primarily a difference in social information processing and sensory experience, rooted in different patterns of neural connectivity. The fundamental difference is in how the brain processes the social and sensory world.

ADHD core traits

  • Difficulty sustaining attention
  • Impulsivity
  • Hyperactivity (physical or mental)
  • Executive dysfunction
  • Emotional dysregulation
  • Time blindness

Autism core traits

  • Social communication differences
  • Sensory processing differences
  • Need for routine and sameness
  • Deep, focused interests
  • Literal thinking
  • Monotropic attention style

Traits that look the same but feel different

Several traits appear in both ADHD and autism but have different underlying causes:

Difficulty paying attention

ADHD: Attention is dysregulated — hard to direct, easy to lose. The brain gravitates towards stimulation and novelty. Tasks that are boring or unimportant are almost impossible to start or sustain.

Autism: Attention is monotropic — intensely focused on one thing at a time. Autistic people can pay deep, sustained attention to areas of interest but struggle to shift attention when required. Difficulty attending to social cues is a feature of how autistic brains process social information.

Social difficulties

ADHD: Social difficulties arise from impulsivity (interrupting, talking over people, saying things without thinking), distractibility (losing track of conversations), and emotional dysregulation (overreacting, difficulty managing conflict).

Autism: Social difficulties arise from differences in processing social information — difficulty reading facial expressions, interpreting tone of voice, understanding implied meaning, and knowing unwritten social rules.

Emotional intensity

ADHD: Emotional dysregulation is a core feature — emotions are felt intensely and managed poorly. Rejection sensitivity (extreme emotional pain in response to criticism or perceived rejection) is particularly common.

Autism: Emotional experiences can be intense and difficult to identify or communicate (alexithymia — difficulty identifying one's own emotions — is common). Emotional regulation can be affected by sensory overload or unexpected changes.

Repetitive behaviours

ADHD: Repetitive or fidgety behaviours (leg-bouncing, nail-biting, doodling) are related to stimulation-seeking and hyperactivity.

Autism: Repetitive behaviours (stimming — hand-flapping, rocking, humming) serve as sensory regulation tools and are a core part of autistic experience.

ADHD vs autism in everyday life

Trait lists can feel abstract. It's often easier to recognise the difference in how each shows up in ordinary moments:

At work

ADHD tends to look like missed deadlines despite good intentions, a desk buried one week and spotless the next, brilliant bursts of hyperfocus followed by days of stalling, and interrupting colleagues before they've finished. Autism tends to look like excelling at detailed, systematic work, struggling with vague instructions or sudden changes of plan, finding open-plan noise and lighting exhausting, and reading an email's tone far more literally than it was meant.

In relationships

ADHD partners may forget important dates, dominate conversations when excited, or feel wounded out of proportion by a mild criticism (rejection sensitivity). Autistic partners may need more alone time to recover from socialising, prefer direct communication over hints, and feel unsettled when routines or plans change without warning.

With rest and downtime

An ADHD brain often can't switch off — it seeks stimulation, scrolls, starts new projects, struggles with boredom. An autistic person frequently needs genuine low-stimulation downtime to recover from the sensory and social demands of the day, and may rely on familiar comfort activities to decompress.

Why both are so often missed in women. ADHD and autism were historically defined using studies of boys, so the diagnostic picture skews towards stereotypically male, outwardly disruptive traits. Many women and girls instead internalise their struggles and learn to mask — copying social scripts, suppressing stimming, forcing eye contact. Masking hides the very traits an assessor is looking for, which is a major reason so many women reach a late diagnosis in adulthood, often after burning out.

Can you have both? — AuDHD

Yes — and it's more common than most people realise. Between 50% and 70% of autistic people also meet criteria for ADHD. The co-occurrence is so consistent that researchers have proposed shared genetic underpinnings. Until 2013, the DSM prevented a dual diagnosis — but current guidance (DSM-5 and ICD-11) explicitly allows both.

When both are present, the combination — informally called AuDHD — creates a distinct experience that's different from having either alone, because the two conditions often pull in opposite directions.

How ADHD and autism are assessed differently

The two conditions are evaluated using different tools, which is part of why being assessed for only one can mean the other is missed entirely.

For ADHD, UK assessments typically start with a brief screening questionnaire such as the ASRS v1.1 (the World Health Organisation's Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale), followed by a structured diagnostic interview like DIVA-5 that looks at symptoms in both childhood and adulthood across different settings.

For autism, NICE recommends the AQ-10 (a 10-question screening tool) as a first step, with fuller assessments drawing on structured interviews and observational tools such as the ADOS-2 or DISCO. These look closely at social communication, sensory experiences and developmental history.

Because the screening tools, interviews and even the clinicians often differ, a service set up to assess ADHD may not be looking for autism, and vice versa. This is why it's worth naming both to your GP if you relate to traits from both columns above. Frequently asked questions

Can ADHD be mistaken for autism?

Yes, and the reverse is also common. Both affect attention and social functioning, so a clinician who doesn't specialise in neurodevelopmental conditions can attribute autistic traits to ADHD or miss one condition when assessing for the other. The underlying mechanisms differ — ADHD is primarily about attention and impulse regulation, autism about social and sensory processing — but on the surface they can look very similar.

Is AuDHD a real diagnosis?

"AuDHD" is an informal term for having both autism and ADHD. It isn't a separate clinical label, but a dual diagnosis of autism and ADHD is fully recognised — the DSM-5 (2013) and ICD-11 both allow the two to be diagnosed together, something earlier manuals did not.

What's the main difference between ADHD and autism?

In one sentence: ADHD is mainly a difference in regulating attention and impulses, while autism is mainly a difference in how the brain processes social information and sensory input. Traits like inattention or social difficulty can appear in both, but for different underlying reasons.

Can you be a little bit autistic or have mild ADHD?

Both exist on a spectrum of impact. Two people with the same diagnosis can be affected very differently, and traits that are manageable in one environment can become disabling in another. What matters clinically is whether the traits significantly affect daily life — not where someone sits on an imagined scale of "severity".
