ADHD time blindness: why time feels different

You meant to leave ten minutes ago. A "quick" task swallowed the whole afternoon. A deadline two weeks away felt like it didn't exist — until it was tomorrow. If time seems to slip through your fingers no matter how hard you try, you may be experiencing time blindness, one of the most under-recognised features of ADHD.

7 minute read  ·  Research-backed  ·  NeuroDiverse People

What is time blindness?

"Time blindness" is the informal term for a difficulty perceiving, estimating, and managing the passage of time. People with ADHD frequently struggle to sense how much time has passed, to judge how long a task will take, and to feel the reality of future events until they are imminent. It is not a lack of caring or effort — it is a genuine difference in how the brain represents time.

The concept was popularised through the work of ADHD researcher Russell Barkley, who argued that ADHD is fundamentally a disorder of self-regulation across time — what he memorably called "time blindness" or "temporal myopia," a near-sightedness for the future. In his model, the ability to hold time in mind and use it to guide behaviour is precisely what ADHD impairs.

The neuroscience — how the ADHD brain processes time

Time perception is not a single sense; it is constructed by the brain from the coordinated activity of several systems — including the prefrontal cortex, the basal ganglia, and the cerebellum — many of which rely on dopamine signalling. These are the same networks implicated in ADHD, which is why time processing is so often affected.

A substantial body of research supports this. A meta-analysis by Nejati and Yazdani, and earlier work reviewed by Noreika, Falter, and Rubia (2013), found consistent differences in time perception between people with ADHD and neurotypical controls across multiple tasks — including estimating durations, reproducing time intervals, and discriminating between lengths of time. People with ADHD tend to perceive time as passing differently and to be less accurate at judging intervals, particularly longer ones.

Dopamine is central to this. The brain's internal "clock" appears to depend on dopaminergic signalling to pace itself; when that signalling is atypical, as in ADHD, the internal sense of time runs less reliably. This links time blindness directly to the same neurochemistry behind other ADHD traits.

"Now" and "not now": Many clinicians describe the ADHD experience of time as binary — there is now, and there is not now. Events in the "not now" category — a deadline next week, an appointment this afternoon — can feel abstract and emotionally weightless until they abruptly collapse into "now" and become urgent. This is why future tasks are so easily forgotten, and why urgency is often the only thing that reliably triggers action.

How time blindness shows up in daily life

Time blindness produces a cluster of experiences that are often misread by others as carelessness or disorganisation:

  • Chronic lateness — despite genuine effort and good intentions, consistently underestimating how long it takes to get ready or travel
  • Losing track of time — looking up from a task to find hours have vanished, or conversely feeling that only minutes have passed
  • Poor time estimation — believing a task will take twenty minutes when it takes two hours, or vice versa (a phenomenon related to the "planning fallacy")
  • The vanishing deadline — a future obligation feeling unreal until it is imminent, then triggering a last-minute scramble
  • Time "collapsing" during hyperfocus — losing all awareness of time while absorbed in something engaging
  • Difficulty with transitions — struggling to gauge when to stop one activity and start another
Why it's not rudeness: People with ADHD who are late or who misjudge time are frequently assumed to be inconsiderate, lazy, or uncaring. In reality they are often more anxious about time than most — because they know from experience that their internal clock cannot be trusted. Understanding lateness as a perceptual difference rather than a moral failing is important both for self-compassion and for the people around them.

Time blindness and task paralysis

Time blindness rarely travels alone. It interacts closely with ADHD paralysis: if a future task feels unreal until it becomes urgent, it is very hard to start it in advance — and then, when it finally becomes "now," the pressure and overwhelm can trigger a freeze. The two together explain a great deal of the last-minute, all-or-nothing pattern many people with ADHD recognise in themselves. Chronic time pressure of this kind is also a well-worn path toward ADHD burnout.

Strategies that genuinely help

Because the internal clock is unreliable, the most effective approaches move timekeeping outside the head, making time visible and concrete.

Make time visible

Analogue clocks, and especially visual countdown timers (such as those that show time elapsing as a shrinking coloured wedge), turn abstract time into something you can see. Many people with ADHD find a visible timer dramatically more effective than a mental estimate.

Externalise deadlines and cues

Use alarms, calendar alerts, and reminders liberally — not as a sign of failure but as a prosthetic for a sense that doesn't run reliably on its own. Set alarms not just for the event but for when you need to start preparing for it.

Time your tasks to learn your real pace

Because estimation is unreliable, gather data: time how long routine tasks actually take. Over time this builds a realistic picture that counters the tendency to under- or over-estimate.

Build in buffers

Deliberately add generous margins around appointments and travel. If you think something will take 30 minutes, plan for 45. Buffers absorb the errors of a faulty internal clock instead of letting them cause lateness.

Body doubling and external structure

Working alongside another person, or borrowing external structure (shared schedules, accountability partners), provides the time-anchoring the internal system struggles to generate — the same reason it helps with task initiation.

If this resonates — what to do next

If time genuinely feels slippery and unreliable to you — and if this has affected your work, relationships, and self-esteem despite real effort — it is worth considering whether ADHD might be part of the explanation. Time-processing difficulty is a core, though often overlooked, feature of adult ADHD.
